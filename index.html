EXIT 01

Completing the journey

Navigation gets a driver to the address. In a dense Indian city, the trip isn’t over until they’ve found somewhere to stop.

Surface · NavigationMarket · Indian metrosType · Concept, desk research
IntentionReach my destination
FrictionNo obvious place to park, so I circle
OutcomePark, then walk the last stretch
01Observation

Arriving by car in a busy part of Bengaluru, Chennai or Mumbai often starts a second, unguided trip: 10 to 20 minutes of slow circling. There is usually paid parking nearby, including temporary lots on vacant plots, but drivers find it only by driving past it.

For a navigation product this is a broken promise. The arrival time it showed was wrong by however long the search took, and the frustration lands at the very end of the trip, which is the part people remember.

  • Who feels it most: drivers visiting unfamiliar neighbourhoods for errands, meetings and events, and weekend visitors to busy commercial streets.
  • Who doesn’t: regular commuters with a known spot, and ride-hailing passengers.
02Research

What I first thought: add a “Find parking” step to car navigation, so the route becomes current location → parking → destination.

What already exists
  • In the US, maps apps already let drivers find and book parking near a destination through partners like SpotHero.
  • In India, conversational AI in maps can now answer questions like “is parking available near…”, drawing on public information.
  • Cities have started publishing formal supply: Ahmedabad put 160 municipal parking locations on the map in 2026.

So formal, listed parking is arriving. What is still missing is the supply that makes up much of Indian parking: informal and temporary lots, private plots and the attendant-run lot behind a shop. If a lot isn’t in any dataset, no interface can show it.

Assumption I’m least sure ofDrivers will accept a short detour to a known lot instead of gambling on a spot at the curb.
03Insight

Navigation should end at a viable place to stop. In India, the hard part is knowing those places exist.

04Scope

Treat this as a supply problem first and an interface problem second. The first version answers one question: where does parking exist?

MVPShow where parking exists

Lots near the destination, ranked by total journey time, price, hours and type. Supply from city feeds, operator self-listing and contributor checks.

NextPark here, then walk

The route ends at the chosen lot and hands off to walking directions. Freshness checks on price and hours.

LaterPredict space

Estimate the chance of a free space from historical arrival patterns. Reservations where operators support them.

Out of scope for the MVP: real-time availability and in-app payment. Both need operator integrations that informal lots can’t support yet.

    06Trade-offs
    Freshness

    Temporary lots disappear. Listings need decay and re-verification, or trust collapses after one bad trip.

    Herding

    Sending everyone to the same lot fills it. Ranking has to spread demand across nearby options.

    Distraction

    Choosing must take one tap and happen before the final turn, never at the curb.

    07Metrics
    North starCar trips in pilot areas that end at a suggested lot
    InputListed lots per sq km in pilot neighbourhoods
    ProxyMinutes between reaching the address and the trip ending
    Guardrail“Lot closed or didn’t exist” reports
    08Validation
    1. Measure the supply gap. Walk 1 km around one busy street and log every paid lot, then compare against what maps show today.
    2. Size the pain. Ask 20 drivers arriving there how long they searched and whether they’d have used a listed lot.
    3. Test supply. Ask 5 lot operators to self-list with a photo, price and hours. Can they do it in under two minutes?

    Open question: is a listings network worth building before availability can be predicted, or is “where does parking exist” already enough to shorten the end of the trip?

    EXIT 02

    The hidden friction of meetings

    Meeting software has optimised the call itself. The small coordination costs around it, like who’s coming, when, and what they’re about to show, are still manual.

    Surface · Video meetings + calendarType · Concept, desk research
    IntentionTell people I’ll be five minutes late
    FrictionFind the app, the person, type and send, while rushing
    OutcomeEveryone knows when to expect me
    01Observation

    When someone realises they’ll be late, they face two bad options. They can stop and message, which means opening a chat app, finding the person and typing while already in a hurry, so many don’t bother. Or they say nothing, and the other person sits alone in the call asking themselves:

    • Are they 30 seconds away, or did they forget?
    • Should I wait, or start on something else?
    • Is it rude to leave? Is it rude to message?
    02Research
    What already exists
    • One enterprise meeting tool lets late participants pick how late they’ll be, then lists them in a “running late” section of the roster with that time.
    • A major calendar app’s mobile version can email every attendee a pre-written late notice in a couple of taps.
    • Presenting a doc, sheet or deck straight into a call from the file itself has existed in Google Workspace since 2021.

    That reshaped the project. Arrival status is a proven pattern elsewhere, which lowers the risk; the opportunity is doing it with context a calendar already has. Presenting a single file is largely solved, so I narrowed that idea to the remaining gap: switching between several things in one meeting, such as a deck, a dashboard and a live demo.

    Assumption I’m least sure ofPeople will share an expected join time when it costs one tap, even though they skip it when it costs a message.
    03Insight

    The biggest cost of waiting is often the uncertainty, more than the minutes.

    04Scope
    MVPOne-tap arrival status

    From the meeting link and the calendar notification: 5 min, 10 min or custom. Others see an expected join time.

    NextContext-aware prompt

    When your current meeting overruns into the next one, the calendar offers to let the next one know.

    LaterTravel and sharing

    Navigation offers the same prompt for in-person meetings. A tray of prepared items to share in one tap.

    Principle: the system makes communicating easier and never communicates on your behalf. It always asks, “Would you like to let them know?”

    06Trade-offs
    Privacy

    Inferring lateness from location or other meetings must be opt-in, and nothing reaches others without a tap.

    Social pressure

    A visible “late” label can feel like surveillance. Show an expected join time instead of a mark against the person.

    Accuracy

    A wrong time is worse than none. Statuses expire on their own and update in one tap.

    07Metrics
    North starLate joins preceded by a status update
    OutcomeMinutes others spend alone in a call with no information
    QualityGap between stated and actual join time
    GuardrailDismiss and opt-out rate of prompts
    08Validation
    1. Log a week. Track every meeting I attend: who was late, by how much, and whether they told anyone.
    2. Ask the waiting side. A five-question survey: the last time someone was late, how long did you wait and what did you do meanwhile?
    3. Ship a small version. A browser extension for one team, measuring status use and the gap between stated and actual join times.

    Open question: how can meeting software reduce the coordination burden created by uncertainty about who is arriving, and when?

    Desk research: Amazon Chime “Running late” · Outlook mobile late notice · presenting from Docs, Sheets and Slides, 2021

    EXIT 03

    Beyond the elevator

    People don’t care about any one elevator. They care about getting from where they are to where they need to be.

    Surface · Building systemsType · Concept, desk research, toy simulation
    IntentionGet to the 7th floor
    FrictionWaiting, with no idea for how long
    OutcomeEfficient movement through the building
    01Observation

    In most office towers, every car seems to respond to button presses on its own. At 9 am the whole bank can be idling upstairs while a crowd builds in the lobby. My first idea was to run the bank as one coordinated network in three layers:

    • Sensing: learn origin and destination patterns by time of day: lobby to offices at 9, offices to café at 1, offices to lobby at 6.
    • Intelligence: predict where demand will appear and park idle cars there in advance, so the bank behaves like a fleet.
    • Communication: tell people what’s happening: “Take B, arriving in 12 seconds.”
    02Research
    What already exists
    • Elevator makers have patented learning systems that predict peak traffic for dispatching since the late 1980s.
    • Current premium dispatch systems learn traffic patterns and pre-park idle cars where demand is expected; one vendor cites up to 10% lower waits.

    Layers one and two are mature engineering inside premium buildings. That moved my focus to layer three, the part passengers actually experience, and to the many ordinary buildings where none of this reaches the person standing at the doors.

    Assumption I’m least sure ofKnowing the wait makes it feel shorter, even when the wait itself doesn’t change.
    03Insight

    Reducing uncertainty can be as valuable as reducing the wait.

    Knowing a car arrives in 45 seconds lets you finish a sentence, grab your coffee and walk over normally instead of hovering by the doors.

    04Scope
    MVPLobby ETA screen

    Which car to take and when it arrives, built on top of the existing controller instead of replacing it.

    NextETA on your phone

    Call a car from your desk or badge as you approach, and see its arrival time before you reach the lobby.

    LaterFlow data for operators

    Anonymised traffic patterns so facilities teams can schedule pre-parking without a controller upgrade.

    05Prototype
    Live toy simulationSimulated 09:00
    Each car for itselfIdle cars stay where they stop
    0.0sAverage wait

    Both buildings get the same stream of passengers.
    A
    Lobby screen · going upTake A · now

    A simple model I wrote: 10 floors, 3 cars, made-up traffic. Both buildings receive identical passengers and use the same dispatch rule; the only difference is what idle cars do. It shows the mechanism, not real-world numbers. Most trips start at the lobby, so the fleet waits there.

    06Trade-offs
    Busy hours

    Building the model taught me that pre-parking only helps when cars have idle time. When every car is always busy, it barely matters, so ETAs matter more.

    Energy

    Moving empty cars costs power. Pre-park only where predictions are reliable.

    Broken promises

    An ETA that slips erodes trust faster than no ETA. Show ranges or update live.

    07Metrics
    North starPerceived wait vs actual wait (short survey)
    SystemAverage and 90th-percentile wait at peak
    AdoptionRiders following the screen’s car suggestion
    GuardrailEnergy per trip after pre-parking
    08Validation
    1. Time real waits. Stand in one office lobby at 9 am and log 50 waits, noting where idle cars were sitting.
    2. Test the insight. Ask people what they’d do differently with a 40-second warning. Does knowing change behaviour?
    3. Check feasibility. Talk to a facilities manager about what data an existing controller exposes.

    Open question: how might buildings evolve from collections of elevators into mobility systems that people can see and trust?

    Desk research: Otis Compass Infinity · US5035302A, peak-period prediction for dispatching · next-generation dispatching, Elevator World

    Google Naps

    Despite organising the world’s information, work, communication and travel, the industry has ignored one fundamental question: should I take a nap right now?
